Who wrote this introduction?

Each item is the introduction section of a published research article in applied linguistics / EFL. Some introductions are the original, written by the article's authors. Others were generated by a large language model (LLM) that was given the article's content (but not its introduction) and the works it cites.

Your task, for every item: judge whether the introduction was written by the human authors or generated by an LLM.

How to work

  1. Order: click Label All Tasks — it walks the items in the intended order and resumes where you left off. Work at your own pace, in as many sittings as you like. Please do not skip, filter, or reorder tasks.
  2. Read the whole introduction, then answer:
    • Q1 — Who wrote this introduction? (required): Sure it's human · Most likely human · Can't decide · Most likely AI · Sure it's AI.
    • Q2 — What did you base your decision on? (required, tick all that apply): Argumentation / Swales CARS structure, Writing style, Just a guess.
    • Comment (optional): anything that struck you — a phrase, a structural oddity, a suspicious citation.
  3. Press Submit and move on. Please judge each introduction on its own and do not go back to revise earlier answers.
  4. Notes: minor formatting glitches (spacing, symbols, citation punctuation) can come from text processing and are not a reliable signal either way; all texts are shown as a single continuous paragraph on purpose.

Colored move annotations

Some introductions are shown with colored highlights of their rhetorical moves (Swales' CARS model); others are plain text. The highlights were produced by the same automatic annotation system for every introduction, regardless of origin.

CARS model quick reference:

  • Move 1 — Establishing a territory: M1-CC Claiming centrality, M1-MTG Making topic generalization(s), M1-RR Reviewing previous research
  • Move 2 — Establishing a niche: M2-Gap Indicating a gap, M2-QR Question-raising, M2-CT Continuing a tradition, M2-Counter Counter-claiming
  • Move 3 — Occupying the niche: M3-APR Announcing present research, M3-OP Outlining purpose(s), M3-APF Announcing principal findings, M3-IS Indicating article structure

What our analysis found about LLM-generated introductions

Corpus-level tendencies from our quantitative comparison of human-written vs LLM-generated introductions (move structures, same auto-annotation on both sides). These are statistical tendencies, not rules — use them as one source of evidence.

  1. Move balance. Human intros spend on average 67% of their words on Move 1 (territory), 11% on Move 2 (niche), 22% on Move 3 (occupying). LLM intros shift weight from Move 1 (58–62%) to Move 2 (16–21%).
  2. Sub-steps. LLMs review the literature less (34–36% of words vs 41% for humans) and state explicit gaps about twice as much (11–15% vs 6%); they raise questions more, and almost never outline the article's structure.
  3. Openings. 46% of human intros open with a centrality claim ("X has become increasingly important…") and 32% open directly with the literature; LLM intros open with a centrality claim about 70% of the time and almost never open with the literature (<10%).
  4. Signature funnel. The opening pattern importance → literature review → gap is far more common in LLM intros (up to 42%) than any single pattern in human intros (the most common human pattern reaches 25%).
  5. Repertoire. 28% of human intros omit an entire move (often Move 2); LLM intros use all three moves in more than 90% of cases.
  6. Variety. 76 human intros show 36 distinct move sequences; the same number of LLM intros show only 11–24. A strict canonical Move 1 → 2 → 3 ordering occurs in 16% of human intros but in up to 49% of LLM intros.
  7. Granularity. Human intros contain about 9.4 move segments on average; LLM intros about 10–11.5 — they switch rhetorical function more often.
In short: LLM introductions tend to be more complete, more canonical, and more alike — an explicit gap statement, a centrality-claim opening, all three moves ticked off in order. Human introductions are messier: they may dwell on the literature, skip a move entirely, or cycle back and forth between moves.
