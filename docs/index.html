Does AI-Generated Scientific Text Follow Human Argumentation Patterns?

A CARS-Based Comparison of Research Article Introductions

Abdelrahman Sadallah, Narjes Sheikh Asadi, Lonneke van der Plas

USI NLP Lab, Università della Svizzera italiana (USI)

Findings of EACL 2027
Paper arXiv Code Data BibTeX

Abstract

Large language models are moving from helping write up research to helping do it, which makes it important to know how the scientific text they produce differs from human writing. Work on this question has stayed mostly at the surface, using lexical and stylistic cues that light paraphrasing erases. We look instead at rhetorical structure, the sequence of argumentative moves through which a text makes its case. We study research-article introductions under Swales' CARS model, and compare original introductions from published linguistics articles with generated counterparts of the same papers. We find that human-written introductions are more flexible in which moves they use and in what order, while the generated ones are more uniform. Giving the models the CARS definitions makes them more rigid.

The CARS model

An introduction has to argue a paper into existence in a few paragraphs. Swales' Create-A-Research-Space (CARS) model describes how: through three rhetorical moves, each realised by smaller steps. Moves may recur, appear in any order, and not every move needs to be present.

Move 1

Establishing a territory

The author shows that the research area matters and reviews what is known.

  • CCClaiming centrality
  • MTGMaking topic generalizations
  • RRReviewing previous research
Move 2

Establishing a niche

The author opens a space for the study, most often by pointing out a gap.

  • GapIndicating a gap
  • QRQuestion raising
  • CTContinuing a tradition
Move 3

Occupying the niche

The author fills that space with the present study.

  • APRAnnouncing present research
  • OPOutlining purpose
  • APFAnnouncing principal findings
  • ISIndicating the structure

Why the introduction? Prior comparisons of human and machine-written text rely on lexical statistics, stylistic fingerprints and syntactic patterns. Such cues say little about how a model builds a text, and light paraphrasing erases most of them.

Rhetorical structure sits below the surface. Introductions are especially dense in rhetorical moves, and the CARS model is the standard account of how they are organised. The figure shows the model at work on the paper's own introduction, cut into consecutive segments that each carry one step.

How we compare

The comparison is paired: every generated introduction is written for a paper whose published introduction we also have, from the same information the author had.

Reconstruct the author's context

Each model receives the paper's title and body, without the introduction, and the works cited in the original introduction with their metadata and abstracts. The target length is the length of the original.

Generate under two prompts

GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemma-4-31B-it and Qwen3.5-27B write every introduction twice: from a basic prompt, and from a CARS-guided prompt that adds the move and step definitions and asks the model to plan the moves first.

Annotate both sides alike

One automatic annotator segments and labels all 684 introductions, original and generated. Using the same annotator on both sides removes the annotator as a confound.

What we find

Human authors vary their structure. The models repeat a few templates.

The models cover the scheme. Human authors select from it.

A human introduction uses on average 2.67 of the 3 moves and 5.3 of the 10 steps, and 28% of the originals leave out an entire move. Most generated sets realise all three moves and 6.1 to 7.4 steps. Where the original establishes no niche, states no explicit gap or never claims centrality, 86 to 96% of its generated counterparts add the missing step. The balance shifts accordingly, from Move 1 toward Move 2.

The models open the same way and keep to the canonical order.

Human authors open with a centrality claim in only 46% of the papers, and another 32% open directly with the literature. The models start with a centrality claim in 58 to 84% of their introductions, and none of the 608 generated introductions opens on Move 3. Each model also has a stronger signature sequence than the human authors, whose most frequent step 3-gram appears in only a quarter of their papers.

Giving the models the CARS definitions makes them more rigid.

The CARS-guided prompt lists the moves and says they may recur and appear in any order. The introductions written under it nevertheless follow the canonical order more closely than the basic ones. For all four models, fewer introductions return to an earlier move and fewer distinct move structures appear. The guidance moves the models toward the template, not toward the human profile.

An annotator close to the human baseline

Expert CARS annotation is slow and hard to scale, and the study needs labels for 684 introductions. We build an LLM-based annotator that partitions an introduction into segments and labels each with its move and step, in one pass over the whole text.

Its prompt embeds the annotation manual the human annotators followed, three worked gold examples and a two-step procedure: decide the move first, then the step, with a one-sentence rationale before each label.

We validate it against a human baseline obtained under identical conditions. Two graduate-student annotators labelled 32 introductions from scratch, and both the humans and the annotator are scored against the expert gold. The annotator reaches 92% of the human step accuracy and is at the human level on moves and boundaries.

Can a reader use the structure?

A student researcher from outside the project judged 120 introductions one at a time, 60 originals and 60 Claude generations of the same papers. The reader was blind to the provenance of each item and had read only the CARS guidelines and a short summary of the corpus-level tendencies.

The reader was correct on 108 of the 120 introductions, and named the argumentation structure as a basis for the decision on 118 of them, against the writing style on 15.

Introductions written with the CARS-guided prompt were the easiest to spot: all 30 were identified as machine-written. Showing the automatic move annotations as highlights made no difference, since the reader did the move analysis from the content either way.

Code and data

  • Master dataset

    76 papers, 684 introductions (76 original, 608 generated), with expert, second-annotator and automatic CARS annotations in one JSON file.

  • Automatic CARS annotator

    Prompts, few-shot demonstrations, a multi-provider client and the validation against the human baseline.

  • Analysis scripts

    Every table of the paper, recomputed from the dataset without an API key or a GPU.

  • Human-study materials

    Design, interface, blinded responses and analysis of the detection study, and the two close-reading apps.

import json

data = json.load(open("data/master_dataset.json"))
paper = data["papers"][0]

for intro in paper["intros"]:
    segments = intro["annotations"]["chatgpt"]["segments"]
    moves = [seg["move"] for seg in segments]
    print(intro["intro_id"], intro["word_count"], moves)

The original introductions and their gold annotations come from the corpus of D'Agostino, Sheikh Asadi and Musi (LREC 2026). The annotation manual is by Narjes Sheikh Asadi.
